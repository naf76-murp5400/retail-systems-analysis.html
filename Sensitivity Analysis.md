## 1. Overview

Retail is financially fragile because its income is directly tied to volatile consumer spending and foot traffic.

The analysis shows that retail value is governed by threshold effects rather than gradual change: once sales or demand fall below key levels, residual land value turns negative quickly, while gains above those thresholds are uneven and difficult to sustain.

Because these thresholds are high and easily disrupted, retail rarely supports land costs on its own.

As a result, retail alone does not generate enough revenue on its own to be viable. It does however, become economically feasible when it accompanies other less volatile elements, such as housing or office real estate, as an amenity.

## 3. Loss Leader Analysis

If retail RLV is negative, apartment rents need to rise about 3 percent to cover losses. This increase is feasible in strong markets where retail boosts amenities or neighborhood quality. Public subsidy may be appropriate when retail provides benefits like walkability or services that help achieve housing and planning goals but aren't reflected in private returns.

## 5. Capital Allocation Reflection

If allocating public funds, retail development should be supported only when it clearly functions as system supporting infrastructure in locations rather than an independent profit center. Public investment is most defensible where retail activates demand, enables housing feasibility, or delivers public goods that the market underprovides on its own. Without these requirements, supporting retail with subsidies may direct limited resources toward areas that have little impact on housing or urban development.
